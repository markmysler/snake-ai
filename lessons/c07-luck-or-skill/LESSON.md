**Question:** How much do runs of the same recipe differ by chance, and can you tell a useless change from a real one?

**Change:** The baseline trained with 5 new seeds, then a 'board-full bonus' of +100 that a snake almost never reaches, also with 5 seeds.

**Your prediction:** The bonus should hardly matter: games that fill the board are rare. Will one of its runs still look better than a baseline run? _Write yours down before you look at the results._

## Background

A seed fixes all the randomness of a run. Comparing two single runs mixes the effect of the change with the luck of the seeds. With several seeds per recipe you see the spread; only ranges that don't overlap show a difference you can trust.

**What to watch:** The seed spread: dots for each seed's test score. Pick the best bonus run and the worst baseline run and compare them: it looks like an improvement.

**Recipes**

- **baseline × 5**: vision short, reward score, Genetic algorithm + Deep Q-learning, 10 min × 5 seeds 100–104.
- **board-full bonus × 5**: vision short, reward apple +10, death -10, win +100, Genetic algorithm + Deep Q-learning, 10 min × 5 seeds 100–104. Changes from “baseline × 5”: reward: + win 100.

**Terms** (explained in the dashboard's Learn tab): Seed, Run-to-run noise, Seed spread, Board-full bonus.

The baseline run for seed 100 is not in the data I was given, so the baseline comparison uses seeds 101 to 104: GA averages 46.6 and DQN 21.0. The GA gap of about +1.3 is well inside the seed spread. The DQN gap is essentially zero.

**Why:** Both agents use the short vision, which only sees the next square, so they cannot plan around their own body. That is why most games end in body crashes. The board-full bonus fires only when the snake fills all 97 apples. This seed won 1 of 100 test games, and the three baseline seeds shown won 0. The GA does reach full-board games during training (best game 97 from about generation 212), so the bonus does occasionally affect the GA's selection, but rarely enough that its effect is smaller than the seed-to-seed noise. DQN sees the bonus even less often, which matches its unchanged score. Note that the four paired differences (same seed, bonus minus baseline) are all positive, from +0.7 to +5.2. Changing the reward changes training from the first generation, though, so pairing by seed does not cancel the noise. With four seeds and a spread of about 4, that pattern is not evidence of an effect.

**What we learned:** One run is one seed, so judge it against the spread across seeds, not against one number. A reward that fires in only about 1 of 100 games moves the score much less than the luck of the seed does.

Its five-seed mean was 20.94 ± 0.8. Its training mean (15.3) is lower than its test score because training moves include random exploration. Its curve rose quickly and then stayed roughly flat between 14 and 17 from minute 1 onward. The bonus runs gave the DQN the same mean (20.94), as expected since it almost never filled the board.

The bonus group's GA five-seed mean was 47.93 (range 41.85–52.52), +1.8 over baseline. Paired by seed, the differences run from −2.4 to +5.2, and with 5 seeds a t-test gives about t≈1.4, which is not distinguishable from zero. The bonus fired in only 2 of 500 bonus test games (one win each on seeds 102 and 103), and no baseline test game was won. Training did reach 97 apples (a full board) in the baseline too, so full boards happen, just rarely.

**Why:** The +100 win bonus is rarely triggered, because filling all 97 free squares on a 10×10 board almost never happens in a test game. Its effect on the GA is therefore on the order of a couple of rare games per 500, which is far below what a single seed can resolve. The observed +1.8 mean difference is the kind of gap you get by chance when each seed's GA ends at a different point of a noisy evolution; the GA's own spread (±2.7) is larger than the effect. The DQN is unaffected for the same reason, and its tiny spread (±0.8) shows it is much more stable than the evolved GA.

The GA is also noisier because its fitness is measured on only 3 games per network, and each generation's winners depend on which seed's random games they happened to get. The DQN ended in body crashes, which suggests a short planning horizon (gamma 0.95 values rewards only about 20 moves ahead) in a game where avoiding one's own tail needs longer foresight. The GA reached about 165 million environment steps in 10 minutes against the DQN's 1.3 million, because the GA's per-step cost is far lower (about 260k steps per second against about 2.5k).

**What we learned:** A single run is one sample. Before you credit a change, compare the mean across seeds and check whether the ranges overlap; picking the best run of one group against the worst of another always makes a useless change look useful.

## What we learned

- **The same recipe varies by chance:** with 5 seeds, the GA baseline scored 43.7–50.1 and DQN 20.1–22.3. Any single run
  sits somewhere in that range.
- **The bonus looked like an improvement for the GA:** 47.9 against 46.2. The best bonus seed (52.5) beat every
  baseline seed. Compared one run with one run, you would have concluded it helped.
- **For DQN the bonus changed nothing at all:** every seed scored exactly what the same seed scored without it. DQN never
  came close to filling the board (its best game: 41 apples), so the bonus was never paid and each seed trained the same
  way.
- **For the GA it was paid, rarely:** its best training game filled the board (97 apples) in every run, with or without the
  bonus, so a few genomes collected the +100. Two bonus seeds also filled the board once in their test games. Whether that
  is an effect or luck, the data can't say: the 1.7-apple gain is far smaller than the spread across seeds.
- **Lesson:** a difference smaller than the spread across seeds is not a result. Check whether the ranges overlap before
  believing a change helped. The verdict in Compare does this for you.
